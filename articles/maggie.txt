Title: Your Future Career Might Not Exist Yet
Byline: By: Maggie Wolff, Data Scientist, Former Marketer, Lifelong Learner
Quote: That's the thing nobody tells you about pivots: they don't always look like a decision.

It’s not just a career path, it’s a career journey. And it’s a lot longer - and potentially a lot more interesting - than you expect.

I’m halfway through my professional career* and I’ve spent most of it in jobs that I didn’t know even existed when I graduated college.

(Halfway through assuming I retire in my mid-60s.)

I graduated high school in 2000, right in the middle of the dot-com boom, so my dream was to become a Webmaster. If you’re not familiar with that term, it’s what we used to call the people who managed websites - programming, publishing, architecture, etc. To support this dream, I declared Computer Science as my major and jumped into CS101 my first semester.

No one told me it was a weed-out course, and well … it weeded me out. Somehow I passed, but I had zero desire for another CS course.

So I did what seemed logical at the time: I went in the opposite direction. Something glamorous and nothing like code. I decided that my new dream was to work at a magazine or do public relations for an arts organization, like a museum, symphony, or ballet company.

I majored in Communications, and my first job after graduation was promoting accounting conferences. Not glamorous or creative.

Lesson Number 1: Most of us will not start our career in a dream job. Also jobs are rarely … dreamy.

## A Step in the Right Direction

My second job was better. Marketing & Public Relations for a hospital system - I was on a small team handling everything from internal communication to media relations to branding and external messaging. I was, at the time, the young millennial on the team, so when Facebook, YouTube, and Twitter started gaining popularity, I got to be the one to figure out what we, as a regional hospital system, should do on those platforms. Partly because no one else "got" social media, and also because no one senior felt it was that serious.

With those digital channels came analytics. Nobody taught me what to do with the data, I was curious and started digging in on my own, no plan behind it other than “let’s see what it’ll tell us.” I started making recommendations to the rest of the team (including the senior folks).

After doing that for a few years, when our organization was merging with another and the marketing team got completely reorganized, I ended up in a brand new role - Online Marketing Manager. This role hadn't existed before, because online previously wasn’t that big of a deal. But now it was, and I was the one figuring out how we were supposed to market our organization online.

I wasn’t even 30 yet and already I was in a job that didn’t exist when I graduated. My undergrad Communications program offered exactly one class in "Online Communication," and it was mostly about chatrooms and listservs. Facebook was invented about 2 months before I graduated from college. And now that was a big part of my job.

## Oops We Did It Again

Years later, at a different company, on a different marketing team (where I had been doing more digital marketing), I was going through yet another department reorganization. This one resulted in a marketing analytics role for me. At first I was apprehensive. I had been doing some basic data analysis in my digital marketing work, but now I was doing it full-time? I enjoyed analysis, but did I enjoy that much? I questioned why my VP would put me in this role and what my other options were. (Her answer: none. It was this or … leave?)

Turns out - I loved it. The universe (and that VP) knew me better than I knew myself, because despite every hesitation, I ended up thriving in analytics more than I ever did in marketing.

That's the thing nobody tells you about pivots: they don't always look like a decision. Sometimes things slowly change over time (or quickly change overnight), and suddenly you realize your career is heading in a direction you didn’t anticipate, or even know existed.
