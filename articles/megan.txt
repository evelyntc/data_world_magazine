Title: From Requirements to Dashboards
Byline: By: Megan Tuano, Business Analyst -> Project Manager
Lead: How Business Analysts Bridge the Gap Between What the Business Wants and What Data Can Show
Quote: A good business analyst treats "I want a dashboard" as the opening line of a conversation, not the final spec.

Every analytics project starts the same way. Someone in leadership says they want "better visibility" into the business, a dashboard gets requested, and a data team gets to work. But somewhere between that first ask and the finished product, a translation has to happen, and that translation is where most analytics projects actually succeed or fail. That's the work of a business analyst, and it's a lot less glamorous and a lot more important than most people realize.

I've spent the last seven years sitting in that gap, working across enterprise Salesforce and Hubspot implementations for clients ranging from Google to federal agencies, and if there's one thing I've learned, it's that stakeholders rarely know what they actually want when they first ask for it. They know they're frustrated. They know they're making decisions with incomplete information. They know they want "a dashboard." What they don't know, and what isn't really their job to know, is what data actually needs to be captured, structured, and displayed to solve the problem underneath the request.

That's where the real work starts, and it almost never starts with a data model. It starts with a conversation. When a stakeholder says they want to track sales performance, my first question is never about fields or objects. It's about the decision they're trying to make. Are they trying to identify underperforming reps, forecast next quarter, or figure out where deals are stalling in the pipeline? Each of those questions leads to a completely different dashboard, even though the initial request sounded identical. A good business analyst treats "I want a dashboard" as the opening line of a conversation, not the final spec.

Once the actual business question is clear, the next challenge is figuring out whether the data even supports answering it. This is the part that catches teams off guard. Someone wants to see conversion rate by campaign, but the campaign field has been inconsistently filled in for two years, or three different teams have been using the same field to mean three different things. Part of the BA's job is surfacing that reality early, before a developer builds a beautiful dashboard on top of broken data. I've sat in plenty of rooms where the hardest conversation wasn't about what to build, but about admitting the data wasn't ready to support the ask yet.

Writing the actual requirements is where the translation gets formalized. This isn't just documentation for documentation's sake. A well-written requirement tells the data or development team exactly what "done" looks like, what fields feed into a metric, how edge cases should be handled, and who the dashboard is actually for. That last part matters more than people think. A dashboard built for an executive who needs a five-second read looks nothing like one built for an operations manager who needs to drill into daily detail. If the BA doesn't capture that distinction up front, you end up with a dashboard that's technically correct and practically useless.

The other piece that tends to get skipped is defining what success actually looks like before anything gets built. It's easy to jump straight into "what fields do we need" without agreeing on the metric that will tell everyone the project worked. I've found that pinning down the specific decision the dashboard needs to support, and the specific number that will change someone's behavior once they see it, saves teams from months of "can you also add" requests after launch. When that alignment happens early, the build phase moves faster and the final product actually gets used instead of quietly ignored after the first demo.

None of this replaces the technical skill of building the dashboard itself. But no amount of technical skill fixes a dashboard that was built to answer the wrong question. The business analyst's job is to ensure that never happens: sitting in the uncomfortable middle between what a stakeholder says they want and what the data can realistically deliver, and keeping questions going until those two things align. It's not the most visible part of an analytics project, but it's usually the difference between a dashboard people open every morning and one that gets built once and forgotten.
